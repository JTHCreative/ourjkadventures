# Manifesto

*An internal document. Our north star, not our marketing copy.*

---

**We are not in the pin business.**

We are in the business of the things people love.

Every person walking down the street is carrying more than anyone can see. The band they can't stop putting on. The trail they want everyone to hike. The city that made them fall in love with cities. A dish they'd cook you on a Tuesday. A book they've pressed into six friends' hands. A team they've loved since they were seven. The tiny thing about themselves they light up when someone else notices.

None of it shows.

That's a small daily loss — walking around full of enthusiasm for things and having no easy way to signal it. The chance meeting that never happens because two people didn't know they had the same favorite album. The stranger in line who would've been a friend if either of you had known you'd both been to the same little town in Portugal.

Humans have solved this forever with crests, patches, band tees, scout sashes, jerseys, bumper stickers. The impulse to wear what you love is older than writing.

We make the modern version of that.

---

## What we actually sell

We sell recognition. We sell the moment someone across a room sees your pin and grins because they know. We sell the smallest possible way to put a piece of what you love on your outside so the right people can find it.

A pin isn't decoration. It's a little flag. It says *this — this is one of mine* — and when someone else who loves the same thing sees it, something happens between two strangers that couldn't have happened otherwise.

That's the product. The pin, and everything that helps it be seen.

---

## The other half of the problem

Pins have always had a quiet second problem, and nobody in the category has bothered to solve it: once you have one you love, where does it go?

The honest answers today are bad. You pin it to a jacket and put a hole in something you paid real money for. You pin it to a hat and it eventually falls off somewhere on the street. You put it in a drawer. You buy one of the display "solutions" on the market — a blank felt square in a shadow box, or a cloth booklet with no design to it, essentially a filing cabinet for things you love.

None of that is worthy of the pin, and none of it is worthy of what the pin points at.

So display and wear are not accessories to our business. They *are* the business, as much as the pins are. Boards designed for a wall you'd actually want to look at. Straps and clasps and keychains and necklaces that let a pin travel with you without wrecking your jacket. Ways to arrange a growing collection so it looks like a collection and not a pile.

If we get the pins right and the display wrong, the pins end up in drawers and we've failed. A pin that isn't seen isn't doing its job.

---

## What follows from this

**The objects have to be good.** People are wearing these because they love the thing the pin points at. If the pin itself is chintzy, we've cheapened what they love. Weight, finish, backing, packaging — all of it should feel like it deserves to sit next to something someone cares about. The same standard applies to everything we make around the pins: a board, a strap, a keychain has to feel as considered as the pin it holds.

**Specific beats generic, always.** A pin for the specific trail, not "hiking." The specific band, not "music." The specific neighborhood, not the city. Love is specific. Generic pins are for people who don't actually love the thing — and those aren't our customers.

**We're warm, not baby-talk.** No "cute!!" No "obsessed!!" The difference is a friend who actually loves the band saying *"oh man, that tour was incredible"* versus a brand performing excitement at you. Enthusiasm from a real person, not from a marketing deck. We talk to adults who are excited about things.

**We are not the joke pin company.** Ironic pins, snarky pins, pins that are making fun of the thing they depict — that's not us. Our whole thing is that we actually love what our customers love. That only works if the pins are sincere. Clever is fine when it's in service of the love. Clever for its own sake makes us interchangeable with a hundred Etsy shops.

---

## The drift to watch for

The pull toward *hollow cute* — pins that are cute for cute's sake, with nothing real underneath — will be constant. It's easier to design. It sells to the impulse market. Every competitor is already there.

Do not follow them.

A pin for *your* team, *your* trail, *your* city works because a real person actually loves the specific thing. A generic cute mushroom pin works for anyone and means nothing to anyone. The first is what we do. The second is what everyone else does.

The day we can't tell the difference, we become a commodity.

---

## The test

Before we ship anything — a pin, a board, a strap, a page, an email, a photo, a caption — one question:

*Would someone who genuinely loves this thing be delighted we made it?*

If yes, ship it. If no, it's not ready.

That's the whole company.
